# Research Design Rationale

## Research Position

Urban Wildlife Simulation is a reflective, time-compressed prototype. It does not predict real animal populations and it is not guidance about feeding wildlife. Its purpose is to make relationships between care, control, dependency, public space, and multispecies survival available for interpretation through play.

The prototype uses productive ambiguity: players see which shared conditions may be affected and the possible magnitude, but not the direction before a decision. The result remains legible after the decision. This preserves uncertainty without confusing interaction feedback.

## Mechanism Audit

| Game mechanism | Research basis | Simulation abstraction | Intended experience | Claim boundary |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Human food improves immediate satiety | Robb et al. (2008); Oro et al. (2013) | One shared food gives species-specific satiety gains | Feeding feels materially helpful in the moment | Values are design parameters, not measured nutritional effects |
| Repeated feeding increases food reliance | Oro et al. (2013); Reynolds et al. (2017) | A slow species-level reliance score rises through repeated successful feeding | Care changes the conditions of future care | The score does not diagnose biological dependency |
| Repeated encounters change willingness to approach | Lowry et al. (2013) | Individual boldness, familiarity, and species-level reliance affect acceptance | Animals are agents that learn, hesitate, or refuse | Short play sessions compress behavioural adjustment and do not model evolution |
| Feeding changes abundance and community composition | Galbraith et al. (2015); Oro et al. (2013) | Feeding momentum, species events, safe group sizes, and carrying capacity alter population | Helping one group can reorganize the whole community | Population thresholds are speculative balancing values |
| Concentrated feeding raises gathering pressure | Becker et al. (2015); Hanmer et al. (2018) | Recent accepted food positions and crowding create delayed contact and competition costs | The location and repetition of care matter | Gathering pressure is not equivalent to infection; health outcomes are context-dependent |
| Infrastructure and management redistribute access | Benitez-Lopez et al. (2010); Pickett et al. (2011) | Corridors, bins, shrubs, fountains, and feeding zones change shared conditions | The city becomes an ecological actor rather than a backdrop | Each policy combines many real processes into one decision |
| Care can also organize and control | Puig de la Bellacasa (2017); Celermajer et al. (2020) | A recurring "Who is training whom?" event appears through attachment to a favorite animal | Players confront care as maintenance, power, and obligation | The event is a critical metaphor, not an ecological finding |
| Delayed and ambiguous consequences | Sengers et al. (2005); Gaver et al. (2003); Hallnas and Redstrom (2001) | Some pressure is accumulated and resolved on later city cycles; previews hide direction | Reflection replaces optimization toward one obvious moral answer | Ambiguity must not hide controls, outcomes, or failure causes |

## Current Design Parameters

- The simulation begins with 21 animals: three individuals from each of seven species.
- A total population of 14-24 is the designed stable band. Crowding pressure begins at 25, high risk begins at 28, and 30 animals sustained for two city cycles exceeds carrying capacity.
- Ten or fewer animals ends a cycle through low population viability. Loss of any species also ends the cycle.
- Natural growth is checked every two city cycles and requires adequate satiety, comfort, and coexistence.
- Repeated accepted feeding can temporarily permit a species to exceed its designed ideal group size by one.
- Reliance, food benefit, safe group sizes, event deltas, and all thresholds are speculative design parameters. They are intentionally legible and tunable, but they are not fitted to field data.

## Evaluation Focus

The prototype should be evaluated through participant interpretation, not only task success. Useful questions include:

1. When did feeding stop feeling like an uncomplicated act of care?
2. Which animal refusals or delayed effects changed the participant's interpretation?
3. Did the four shared conditions feel like competing values or merely scores to maximize?
4. How did attachment to a favorite animal affect decisions about the wider community?
5. Which outcomes felt meaningfully ambiguous, and which felt merely unclear?

Iteration notes should record the rule changed, the assumption it exposes, participant interpretations, unexpected behavior, and the resulting design decision. This treats the prototype as Research through Design rather than as a finished ecological model.
